Mohr's circle calculator

Enter a plane stress state to get principal stresses, maximum shear stress, the principal angle and von Mises stress, with the circle drawn to scale.

—σ2 (MPa)
—principal angle θp

—von Mises (MPa)
—σx′ at θ (MPa)
—σy′ at θ (MPa)
—τx′y′ at θ (MPa)
X (σx, τxy) and Y (σy, −τxy)rotated element X′, Y′principal stresses
How it works

Centre C = (σx + σy)/2, radius R = √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²]. Principal stresses σ1,2 = C ± R, principal angle tan 2θp = 2τxy / (σx − σy).

On a rotated element: σx′ = C + (σx − σy)/2·cos 2θ + τxy·sin 2θ and τx′y′ = −(σx − σy)/2·sin 2θ + τxy·cos 2θ.

Sign convention: tension is positive, θ is anticlockwise, τ is plotted upward with X at (σx, τxy). Turning the element by θ moves X by 2θ clockwise around the circle. Von Mises for plane stress: σvM = √(σx² − σxσy + σy² + 3τxy²).

How it works

Euler–Bernoulli beam theory, linear elastic, small deflections. Maximum deflection: cantilever end load PL³/3EI, cantilever distributed wL⁴/8EI, simply supported centre load PL³/48EI, simply supported distributed 5wL⁴/384EI.

Bending stress σ = M·c / I with c the distance to the outer fibre. Second moments: rectangle bh³/12, round bar πd⁴/64, tube π(D⁴ − d⁴)/64.

Shear deformation, stress concentrations at supports and holes, and buckling are not included.

What you're seeing

Each element is a spring with stiffness k = EA/h. The page assembles them into one matrix, applies the fixed support, and solves K u = F for the node displacements.

The nodal displacements are exact for this problem, but the stress inside each element is constant, so the peak stress at the support is underestimated by 1/(2n). Doubling the elements halves the error. This is why every serious FEM study includes a mesh convergence check.

Fatigue crack growth life (Paris law)

Estimate how many load cycles a crack takes to grow from its initial size to the critical size where the part fractures.

—cycles to failure
—critical crack size (mm)
—stress range Δσ (MPa)
—ΔK at start (MPa√m)
—ΔK at failure (MPa√m)
—cycles to double a₀
—

Crack length against cycles. Most of the life is spent while the crack is still small.

How it works

Paris law da/dN = C (ΔK)^m with ΔK = Y Δσ √(πa). Integrating from a₀ to the critical size a_c = (1/π)(K_IC / (Y σmax))² gives the life in closed form.

Δσ counts only the tensile part of the cycle (σmin below zero is taken as zero). Constant Y, no threshold ΔKth, no load sequence effects. Steel constants are the Barsom–Rolfe values. Use test data for your own material when you design a real part.

Composite stiffness calculator

Stiffness and density of a unidirectional fibre composite from its fibre volume fraction, with a comparison against aluminium and steel.

—E₁ along fibres (GPa)
—E₂ across, Halpin–Tsai (GPa)
—E₂ inverse rule, lower bound
—density (g/cm³)
—E₁/ρ (GPa per g/cm³)
—vs aluminium (E/ρ ≈ 25.6)
E₁ along fibresE₂ across fibres (Halpin–Tsai)
How it works

Along the fibres the phases share the strain: E₁ = Vf·Ef + (1 − Vf)·Em. Across them the inverse rule 1/E₂ = Vf/Ef + (1 − Vf)/Em is a lower bound; Halpin–Tsai with ξ = 2, E₂ = Em(1 + ξηVf)/(1 − ηVf), η = (Ef/Em − 1)/(Ef/Em + ξ), is closer to test data.

Carbon fibres are anisotropic, so their transverse modulus (about 15 GPa) is used for E₂. Values are typical; ply properties from your supplier's datasheet should be used for design.

Material selector (Ashby chart)

Pick a design goal and see which materials do it best. The line on the chart passes through the best candidate; everything above it performs better for that goal.

Best for this goal

Typical room-temperature values. Strength is yield for metals, tensile for polymers, composites and wood, flexural for technical ceramics and compressive for concrete. Tmax is the melting point, solidus, softening or glass transition. Grades and suppliers vary, so use datasheets for design. Click a column to sort.

How it works

For a light beam of fixed length and stiffness, the mass is smallest when E^½/ρ is largest (Ashby, Materials Selection in Mechanical Design). On log axes every index is a straight line: slope 1 for ties, 2 for beams and 3 for plates.

Peak positions only. Heights are equal here; real intensities depend on the structure factor, multiplicity and texture.

How it works

Cubic spacing d = a / √(h² + k² + l²), Bragg's law λ = 2d sin θ. Allowed reflections: BCC when h + k + l is even; FCC when h, k, l are all odd or all even; diamond cubic when all odd, or all even with h + k + l divisible by 4.

Diffusion and creep life calculator

Diffusion coefficients from the Arrhenius equation, and creep-rupture life at service temperature from a test result using the Larson–Miller parameter.

—Larson–Miller parameter
—predicted life (hours)
—predicted life (years)

D against temperature on a log scale. A rise of 100 °C often speeds diffusion tenfold or more.

How it works

Arrhenius: D = D₀ exp(−Q/RT) with R = 8.314 J/mol·K and T in kelvin. Characteristic diffusion distance x ≈ √(Dt). Preset D₀ and Q are textbook values (Callister).

Larson–Miller: LMP = T (C + log₁₀ t_r) with T in kelvin and tr in hours, C ≈ 20 for many steels. At the same stress, equal LMP means equal damage, so a short hot test predicts a long cooler service life. Extrapolating far beyond test data is risky; use it for screening.

Concepts, software and where they're used

Short explanations of the ideas behind the tools, a guide to the software engineers actually use, and a quick quiz to test yourself.

Stress, strain and stiffness

σ = E·ε

Stress is force per unit area and strain is the relative change in length. In the elastic range they are proportional, and Young's modulus E measures stiffness. Steel is about three times stiffer than aluminium.

Compare materials →

Stress transformation

σ₁,₂ = (σx+σy)/2 ± R

The same stress state looks different on differently oriented planes. Principal stresses are the largest and smallest normal stresses and act on planes with no shear. Mohr's circle shows every orientation at once.

Try Mohr's circle →

Yield criteria

σvM = √(σ₁² − σ₁σ₂ + σ₂²) ≤ σy

Ductile metals yield when the distortion energy reaches a limit (von Mises) or when the maximum shear stress does (Tresca). That's why FEM results are usually shown as von Mises stress.

Calculate von Mises →

Beam bending

σ = M·y / I

Bending stress grows linearly from the neutral axis. Stiffness depends on E·I, and I grows with the cube of the section height, which is why I-beams and tubes put material far from the centre.

Try the beam calculator →

The finite element method

K·u = F

FEM splits a part into small elements with simple behaviour, assembles their stiffness into one matrix and solves for the displacements. Finer meshes converge to the exact answer, and checking that is part of every good study.

See FEM converge →

Fatigue and fracture

da/dN = C·ΔK^m

Repeated loads grow cracks a little every cycle, even far below the yield stress. Growth follows the Paris law until the stress intensity reaches the fracture toughness KIC and the part breaks.

Estimate fatigue life →

Creep

ε̇ = A·σⁿ·exp(−Q/RT)

Above about 0.4 of the melting point in kelvin, metals slowly stretch under constant load. Turbine blades, boiler tubes and solder joints are all designed against creep.

Estimate creep life →

Diffusion

D = D₀·exp(−Q/RT), x ≈ √(Dt)

Atoms move by jumping into vacancies or between lattice sites. The rate rises steeply with temperature, and it controls heat treatment, carburising, sintering and creep.

Calculate diffusion →

Crystal structures and XRD

nλ = 2d·sin θ

Most metals are FCC, BCC or HCP. X-rays reflect from planes of atoms only at angles set by Bragg's law, so a diffraction pattern identifies the phase and its lattice parameter.

Predict XRD peaks →

Composites

E₁ = Vf·Ef + Vm·Em

Stiff fibres in a light matrix give very high stiffness per weight along the fibres but much less across them. Laminates stack plies at different angles to balance this.

maximise E^½/ρ for a light, stiff beam

Ashby's method turns a design goal into a performance index. Plotting materials on property charts shows the best candidates at a glance.

Density functional theory

Kohn–Sham: [−½∇² + V_eff] ψᵢ = εᵢ ψᵢ

DFT calculates a material's energy from quantum mechanics without fitted parameters. It predicts lattice constants, elastic constants and defect energies that feed larger-scale models.

See DFT projects →

Molecular dynamics

mᵢ·aᵢ = −∇ᵢU(r₁…r_N)

MD moves every atom according to forces from an interatomic potential. It reveals mechanisms such as dislocation motion and grain-boundary sliding over nanoseconds.

See MD projects →

Parametric CAD

features + constraints + parameters

Parametric CAD stores how a part is built, not just its shape. Change one dimension and the whole model updates, which makes design iterations and simulation much faster.
